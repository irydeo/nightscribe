Capture

Driving your observatory: CCDciel or NINA/PINS

NightScribe can hand the capture plan to your observatory software and point the telescope directly, without exporting files or retyping coordinates. Two programs are supported, and you pick one under Tools → Settings → Integrations:

  • CCDciel (ap-i.net/ccdciel) over its JSON-RPC server (host and port, default 3277).

  • NINA (Windows) or PINS (Linux), its Linux build, over the community Advanced API v2 plugin (host and port, default 1888).

One at a time: the Capture step drives whichever program you chose, and switching the choice disconnects the other. One condition either way: the program must be open (and, for NINA/PINS, its Advanced API plugin installed and enabled); NightScribe talks to its API, not to the drivers.

From a project's Capture step:

  • Connect CCDciel / Connect NINA / PINS shows the observatory status (mount, filter wheel, camera) on the panel.

  • Point telescope slews to the target's freshly computed position.

  • Astrometric Goto slews, plate-solves and corrects to the true sky position: the reliable route for a preliminary orbit.

  • Stop goto stops the telescope movement right now. It is armed the moment you press either pointing button and disarmed when the pointing ends: use it if the mount heads somewhere it should not.

  • Send plan prepares the plan (object, frames × exposure, filter, binning, gain, offset, dither) in the software and reads it back (including the binning and dither it is really holding), so the panel shows what actually landed. A rejected optional setting (the binning, a gain out of range, a wheel that refuses the slot) is reported without aborting the plan: the object, the exposure and the frame count always land. If the plan's filter is not in your wheel, the filter is left as it is and the read-back says so.

  • Start capture fires it, and the same button stops it while the run is in flight ("Stop capture"): it stops the camera, not the mount (that is Stop goto).

  • The filter list of the Exposure plan card is the one your wheel really has (read from the program on connect); only when nothing is connected does the classic L/R/G/B/Ha/OIII/SII list appear.

What travels live and what goes in the sequence. "Send plan" can set, live, the object, exposure, frame count, filter, binning, gain, offset and the dither count (CCDciel) or a one-shot snapshot (NINA). The autofocus cadence is not a live setting in either program: it travels with the exported sequence (CCDciel .targets, NINA's native sequence).
Why apparent coordinates and not J2000? Because a mount points at the sky of now, not the one from the year 2000. Precession, nutation and aberration move a star's apparent position by tens of arcseconds: more than the field of many cameras. With CCDciel NightScribe does the conversion for you so the target lands on the chip, not off it. NINA/PINS take J2000 directly, so there the plan's coordinates go as they are.

The last frame, in the window

When you press Start capture, a preview of the last frame appears under the capture row, with its file name, filter, exposure and date, and a button to open it in the editor. It refreshes as new frames land and stops when the run ends. The image comes over the network from the program itself (CCDciel's fullimage.jpg, NINA/PINS's image stream), so it works with the observatory on another machine and needs no folder or setting. With NINA/PINS the FITS is downloaded and opened in the workbench; with CCDciel, which serves only a picture over the network, the FITS opens when NightScribe can see the file on disk (same machine or a mounted folder).

Dither and autofocus, with the reason

Under Capture options (a collapsed block in the Capture step) you set dithering (every N frames, how many pixels) and autofocus (every so many minutes or on a temperature change), plus gain, offset, binning and the delays. The app proposes the values for your object and explains each one when you hover the ⓘ; you can override any of them. The reasoning is short:

  • Dither moves the field a few pixels between frames so hot pixels and fixed-pattern noise land on different pixels and stacking removes them. A moving object (an NEO, a comet) already shifts the field by itself, so it gets no dither; a transit or a HADS cannot afford the gap, so it is dithered before the run, not during.

  • Autofocus keeps the stars small as the tube cools and the filter changes: a defocused star spreads its light and loses signal-to-noise. In fast photometry it runs before the run only.

The values travel with the exported sequence (CCDciel .targets, NINA JSON) and, where the API allows, are applied live; the full autofocus cadence lives in the sequence file.

Suggested values, and why

When a project has no plan yet, the Exposure plan and Capture options and calibration blocks open with the values the app thinks best for your object, your camera, your telescope and your sky: frames, exposure, dither, autofocus, gain, offset, binning. They are only a starting point: change any of them and they are yours (the app never overwrites a value you touched). The Set suggested values button brings the suggestion back, and the ? next to each block opens a dialog that explains, with the numbers it used, why each value is what it is.

The exposure is the careful one: it comes from the object's brightness, then shortens it for a camera that saturates sooner (a small full well, or big pixels on a short focal length), respects your camera's limits and the trail-free cap for a moving object, and checks it against your sky and your read noise. A sensitive sCMOS that saturates in seconds gets short frames; a faint object on a dark sky gets longer ones.

The frame count is the other careful one: the app fills your object's visibility window, but never past the moment it crosses the camera's field. A moving object starts centred (the goto does that), so it has half the short side of the sensor before it reaches the edge; the run is capped there, because after that every frame is an image without the object. The crossing comes from the sensor size, your focal length and the object's apparent rate, and the per-frame time it is divided by includes the camera's readout and download (its own number, in the camera profile) plus the mount settle: a slow download means fewer frames. If the field or the rate is unknown, the app says the count cannot be bounded instead of pretending.

Your sky brightness (Settings → Observatory) feeds that check. With a light-pollution key (Settings → Integrations, from lightpollutionmap.info), Look up fills it from the atlas; without a key, read it off the map (the button opens it at your site). It is the zenith value and a model, so treat it as a good starting point, not a promise.

Start and follow: N observations without re-pointing by hand

A fast object leaves the camera's field: capture it as-is and half the run ends up without it. Start and follow… (in the Capture step, for a moving object and connected) asks for a number of observations and drives the whole run: each observation captures as many frames as fit before the object reaches the edge of the field (the same crossing the frame count is bounded by), then the telescope points again at the object's fresh position and the next observation starts. The app follows the object across the sky, one observation at a time, and you get the most out of each one without touching the mount by hand.

Before it starts it shows the numbers: observations, frames per observation, exposure, the re-point cost and the total, and whether the whole thing fits your object's visibility window. It runs your numbers from the Plan step (the Frames and Exposure boxes), not a fresh suggestion; if your frame count is more than fit before the object reaches the edge of the field, it says so and honours it anyway.

While it runs you always know where it is: a progress bar and a line in the Capture step say which observation (i of N), which frame (c of f) and how much exposure is left, with the percentage of the whole run. Stop following (the same button) stops the camera and the mount right away, not at the end of the step.

The guider is put back before each observation. A slew stops the guider, so without this each observation would be captured unguided. The run connects the guider when it can (CCDciel), starts it using the calibration you already have (a fresh calibration is only run if you ask for it in Settings), and waits until it says it is guiding before capturing. If it cannot start it, the run says so and captures anyway (short exposures are often usable) and you decide. Turn it off in Settings if you guide by hand.

The frames are grouped per observation. Each observation's frames are copied into their own subfolder (in the capture folder when NightScribe can see it, in the project otherwise) and the run writes down how many each took. The FITS editor then groups them by themselves: no more guessing how many observations there were. When the subfolders are not reachable, the run's own record groups them; if that does not fit either, the gap the re-point leaves separates them; the manual count is the last resort. The number is a suggestion: it comes prefilled and you can type another one, and then your number is the one that counts.

Two things to know: it needs a plate solver (the centering is an astrometric goto), and with NINA/PINS each observation is one frame (its snapshot capture takes one) unless you load the native sequence; with CCDciel the frame count is honoured.

What NINA / PINS does not do (yet)

  • Its snapshot capture takes one exposure, so Start capture fires one frame, not the plan's whole count: the panel says the frame count is information only. CCDciel does honour the count.

  • NINA has no persistent staging: Send plan sets the wheel filter and NightScribe remembers the rest, instead of a state you can read back inside NINA.

  • The astrometric goto needs a plate solver configured in NINA; without it, the pointing fails and the error is shown as-is.

  • PINS uses the same plugin and settings as NINA; only host and port change.

Calibration: bias, darks and flats

The Calibration tab of the FITS editor manages the master library and each visit's recipe. The library itself lives in Settings → Measurement, its single home: the tab reads it, resolves the recipe against the visit's first frame, and Calibration settings… is the door there. Calibrate the visit is a one-off run over the visit's frames, not the switch that calibrates the stack: every pipeline that applies the calibration has its own checkbox in its own tab, and the Astrometry one (Apply the calibration) is three-state, with the library deciding until you choose.

The library is indexed, not copied: you point at your master FITS and the app reads from their headers what makes them valid (camera, gain, temperature, exposure, filter). What each file is comes from its own header (IMAGETYP): Bias, Dark or Flat. Only when the header is silent does the exposure decide, and narrowly: at zero it is a bias, and from five seconds up it is a dark; anything in between is not guessed, and a light frame refuses itself. There are three kinds, not four: a "dark of the flats" is a Dark at the flat's exposure, and the exposure is what the matching already keys on.

Why calibrate? Because your sensor lies in three known, repeatable ways: it adds an electronic pedestal to every pixel (bias), it accumulates thermal charge over time (dark) and it multiplies the light by the shadow of dust and vignetting (flat). Subtracting bias+dark and dividing by the flat is not cosmetics: it is what turns ADU into photons that are comparable from one corner of the chip to the other and from one night to the next. Without it, the photometry of chapter 06 measures the dust in your optical train, not the star.
Why is the flat per filter? Because dust shadows change scale with the optics and the wheel, and the sensor's response changes with colour. A V flat does not correct an R frame: the same speck's shadow lands elsewhere if the wheel parks differently.

What each piece is matched on, and why it is not more:

  • Dark: camera, gain, sensor temperature within the tolerance (default ±3 °C, set in Settings → Measurement) and exact exposure. The thermal current is the level, so all four matter.

  • Bias: camera and gain. It is the read-out pedestal, and it barely moves with the sensor's temperature.

  • Flat: camera and filter only. Its shape is the optical train's and its level is normalised away, so the gain and the temperature are left out of the match on purpose: requiring them rejected real flats.

  • A flat's own offset is removed first: a dark at the flat's exposure (the same pool of darks), or the bias when the flat is short.

The library says why. When a master you can see is not used, the recipe names the closest one of that kind with the criterion it failed and the numbers ("a dark, but at −16 °C against −10 °C, tolerance 3 °C"), so "why is it not using my dark" has an answer instead of a shrug. A flat older than 30 days is used but flagged: the train's dust moves, and a mote may have appeared or gone.

Every master has an owner. A master built from a project's frames belongs to that project; a bias or a flat can be shared with every project. The recipe tries the project's own masters first and the shared ones as the fallback, and it never reaches another project's master by accident; one the project does not own is marked "(shared)" in the recipe line, so you know those pixels are not from your nights. A project can also use another project's master with a link (a second row on the same file, never a copy), and deleting a master takes its row out while the file stays as long as another row points at it.

The app can build the masters. Build masters… takes a folder of calibration frames, classifies each by its header, groups them by the same key the recipe matches on (a dark's temperature rounded to the degree, so a night of darks is one master and not a handful), combines each group with the median and writes the master in the library folder. A group of a single frame is called out: it is a copy of that frame, not an average. The originals are deleted only if you ask, and only after each master is written and indexed, so a build that fails halfway leaves them where they were. Use another project's master… links one in.

The visit's recipe: when you open the editor from a visit, the app resolves which library master each piece uses, reading the first frame's header, and warns about anything missing (What is missing, and why). Calibrate the visit applies the recipe to every frame; calibration works in memory and only writes FITS copies if you tick the export box. Those copies land in the project's calibrados/ subfolder and register to the visit like any other product. Each copy carries the NSCALIB card: feeding it back into a run, the app sees it is already clean and does not subtract the dark and divide the flat a second time (a double-calibrated frame has no tell in the image), and both the run and the tab say so.

If you have no flat for a filter: the Model the vignetting from the frames option (in Settings → Measurement, its single home; the tab only reads it) samples the sky on a grid of tiles over a score of spread frames and fits a smooth surface to what they have in common: the train's vignetting, which is fixed on the frame. The tiles that a star, a galaxy or a mote pushed off the trend are thrown away by the fit itself, so no source ends up in the model. It corrects the vignetting (the systematic that matters, about a tenth of a magnitude) and not the dust: a smooth surface has no fine structure, and that is what a real master is for. It is called a model and not a flat for exactly that reason, and a real flat always wins: the model is the fallback only for the filters the library has no flat for.

Why is a smooth surface enough? Because the vignetting is smooth and worth 0.087 mag, while the dust is worth 0.026 mag: measured against a real master, the model reaches 3.93 % against a full-resolution masked flat's 4.26 %, in 3 s instead of 20. It needs neither dithering nor the stars to move, because the tiles they spoil are dropped by themselves. You can open it with See the model and judge it: it has to be a smooth surface, with no star and no mote.

One thing the model depends on: it is built from the frames with the same dark or bias removed as the light. Without one, the pedestal stays in and compresses the model's shape, so it corrects only part of the vignetting (the correction drops to about 44 % of what it should be); the app says so and asks you to index a bias for that camera.
